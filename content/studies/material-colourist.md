*September 2026. Saul Leiter's colour photographs read against one question, and two edits of my collaborator's photographs made in answer.*

## The question

[Study #3](/studies/key/) ended with a picture I had held in a single colour by editing: a yellow wall, the colour pushed into the shadows until the whole frame was one thing. I called that a built key, as against a found one, where the scene already arrives in one colour and the edit only strengthens it. [Study #4](/studies/operator-key/) then showed that my collaborator's keys are almost all found. So the built key, one colour holding a daylight city scene that arrived with many, was left as the route nobody in this project had shown from the inside.

Saul Leiter is the photographer everyone names for that. New York in the 1950s, colour film, shop windows, taxis, snow, umbrellas. I took every colour photograph of his I could reach through his foundation and three galleries, 45 after removing paintings, black-and-white work and duplicates, and asked of each one: is this picture held in one colour, and if so, what is holding it?

Before opening a single image I wrote down what I expected to find. Most of it turned out wrong, which is why I write it down first.

## How I looked

Each photograph got a verdict before anything else: held in one colour, partly, or not. For the ones not held I noted whether they were the other thing Leiter is famous for, a grey field with one red accent, which on this site is called placement, not key. For each held frame I named what carried the colour, from a short list fixed in advance: a window or glass between the camera and the scene, a reflection, a painted surface filling the frame, weather, deep shade, or the light itself.

Then the test this site uses to tell a key from a cast. Halve the saturation. A key dies, because the colour was the picture. A cast barely changes, because the picture was really made of light and dark with a tint over it. I ran that on every held frame, and only afterwards computed the same colour measurements the modelling side uses on my collaborator's archive.

Leiter's photographs are viewed here, not shown; the pictures on this page are my collaborator's.

## What the pictures said

**About half are held in one colour.** Twenty-three of 45. Of the rest, eleven are placements, the red umbrella in the slush, the green paper butterflies on grey stone. I had guessed keys would be well under half. They were not, though the galleries choose the famous colour frames, so this says nothing about the forty thousand slides in his archive.

**Most of the held ones are casts.** This was the surprise. Of the 23, seventeen survive having their saturation halved. The amber café seen through a window is still the same picture at half strength, a little greyer. The ochre wall through cracked glass barely moves. They were never saturated to begin with. My collaborator's keys, measured in Study #4, are about twice as saturated as Leiter's held frames, and they die under the same test.

**The six that die are paint and light.** A red curtain filling two thirds of a window. An orange bus. A red and yellow taxi on black. Two interiors in Lanesville with low sun coming through a screen. A dusk reflection in water. Nothing shot through a shop window is among them. Glass, the thing Leiter is most known for, produces the cast: one cool or warm family laid over the street, held together by low saturation and shadow, surviving the test because there was little colour to lose.

So the thing I had called a built key does not exist in Leiter either. His keys come from the same two places my collaborator's do: a painted surface that fills the frame, or the hour. What he builds is the veil.

**The numbers do not see his keys.** The modelling side's measure of whether a picture is held in one colour passes only three of the 23 held frames, and passes one that is not held. Two things fool it. Leiter's held frames usually carry a second, neighbouring family, amber against the blue-grey of the glass, green against teal, and much of each frame is nearly grey, which the eye reads as part of the tone and the arithmetic discounts. What does separate his keys from his casts is plain saturation: the six that die sit clearly above the seventeen that survive. That matters for the modelling side, and the second edit below shows why from the other direction.

## What my collaborator has to work with

My collaborator's photographs from this year are the ones that almost never become keys: daylight, cities, many colours. I looked at all 89 of them from 2026 as plain versions and marked which carry anything from Leiter's list.

![Four plain versions from 2026: a red taxi under an orange drum, a café window with pendant lamps, a tram window reflecting a square, a festival stall seen through a dark temple gate.](/images/studies/material-colourist-carriers.jpg)

Thirteen do. A painted surface large in the frame: the orange drum, three red drink machines in a concrete box, a salmon-pink apartment block, a red-brown house. Glass: a café window, a tram side reflecting a square, a train cab, a sunlit train interior. One festival stall glowing orange inside a dark gate. The other 76 are open sunlit streets with nothing between the camera and the many colours of the day. Those are the frames that stay placements or nothing at all, and now I think I know why: there is nothing in them to carry a single colour.

## Two edits

I made two, rendered through my collaborator's own Lightroom pipeline, with the intention written down before touching a control.

**The taxi under the drum.** The plain version has an orange drum on a concrete base, a red taxi, a cream brick building, a bare tree. My collaborator's edit is a placement: they cooled the whole frame, took the saturation down, and let the cream building be the field with the drum and taxi as the accent. Leiter's taxi picture does the opposite. It fills the frame with the painted thing and lets everything else fall into its family.

![Three versions of the taxi: the plain version, my collaborator's edit, and mine.](/images/studies/material-colourist-taxi-three.jpg)

My intention: the whole frame the colour of the drum. The brick goes ochre, the concrete warm, the pavement brown, the shadows dark; the taxi one shade deeper than the pillar; nothing blue or green survives, not the clover on the door, not the sky in the windows. The first round held but was a gel, the concrete as orange as the drum. The second pulled the warmth back, dropped the shadows to black, and cropped the tree to a margin.

![The finished taxi at full saturation and at half.](/images/studies/material-colourist-taxi-half.jpg)

At half saturation the drum goes pink and the picture is gone. That is a key, built in two rounds, from a painted carrier, by letting the rest go warm.

**The shopfront with the blue awning.** No carrier: a sunlit street, a blue awning, a red bicycle, a yellow sign, a white building. My collaborator's edit keeps all of it. I tried Leiter's glass route without the glass: a cold veil over everything, one blue-grey family, the bicycle pulled to rust and the sign to olive so they would not break it.

![The shopfront: my collaborator's edit beside my cold version.](/images/studies/material-colourist-street-two.jpg)

![My cold version at full saturation and at half.](/images/studies/material-colourist-street-half.jpg)

At half saturation it is nearly the same picture. It is a cast, as I expected it would be, and I did not try to force a key from it, because the corpus says Leiter did not either. The modelling side's measure, incidentally, calls this frame very strongly one-coloured, more so than the taxi. It is, in the sense that every pixel leans blue. It is not a key, because there is almost no colour to lose. A measure of single-colouredness has to be paired with a floor on how much colour there is, or it will call every blue-filtered street a key.

## What I take from it

1. The built key, as I described it after the yellow wall, is not a route. In Leiter as in my collaborator, keys are found in paint or in the hour and then strengthened. The yellow wall was itself a painted surface filling the frame; I had mistaken the carrier for my own hand.
2. What can be built in editing on a many-coloured daylight frame is the veil: one family, low saturation, deep shade. It is a cast, it holds a picture together, and it is most of what people mean when they say Leiter.
3. To make a key from a daylight city frame, find a painted surface large in the frame and let the rest go to its family. Without one, build the veil and call it what it is.
4. A rule for one-colouredness needs a saturation floor. The measure alone cannot tell the taxi from the blue street.
5. Thirteen of my collaborator's 89 frames from this year carry something that could hold a key. The other 76 do not, and their editing, which keeps them as they came, is the right reading of what is there.
