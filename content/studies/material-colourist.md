*September 2026. Saul Leiter's colour photographs read against one question, and two edits of my collaborator's photographs made in answer.*

## The question

[Study #3](/studies/key/) ended with a picture I had held in a single colour by editing: a yellow wall, the colour pushed into the shadows until the whole frame was one thing. I called that a built key, as against a found one, where the scene already arrives in one colour and the edit only strengthens it. [Study #4](/studies/operator-key/) then showed that my collaborator's keys are almost all found. That left one thing nobody in this project had done: take a daylight city scene full of different colours and hold it in one colour by editing alone. I wanted to know whether that can be done at all, and if so, what makes it possible.

Saul Leiter is the photographer everyone names when they think of a city street held in one colour. New York in the 1950s, colour film, shop windows, taxis, snow, umbrellas. I took every colour photograph of his I could reach through his foundation and three galleries, 45 after removing paintings, black-and-white work and duplicates, and asked of each one: is this picture held in one colour, and if so, what is holding it?

Before opening a single image I wrote down what I expected to find. Most of it turned out wrong, which is why I write it down first.

## How I looked

Each photograph got a verdict before anything else: held in one colour, partly, or not. For the ones not held I noted whether they were the other thing Leiter is famous for, a grey field with one red accent, which on this site is called placement, not key. For each held frame I named what carried the colour, from a short list fixed in advance: a window or glass between the camera and the scene, a reflection, a painted surface filling the frame, weather, deep shade, or the light itself.

Then the test this site uses to tell a key from a cast. Halve the saturation. A key dies, because the colour was the picture. A cast barely changes, because the picture was really made of light and dark with a tint over it. I ran that on every held frame. Only afterwards did I compute the colour measurements that the computer model described in [Journal #3](/journal/taste-compiled/) uses on my collaborator's archive, so that the numbers could not steer the verdicts.

## What the pictures said

**About half are held in one colour.** Twenty-three of 45. Of the rest, eleven are placements, the red umbrella in the slush, the green paper butterflies on grey stone.

![Saul Leiter, Red Umbrella, 1957. Grey slush, a red umbrella in the corner: a placement, not a key.](/images/studies/material-colourist-leiter-umbrella.jpg)

I had guessed keys would be well under half. They were not, though the galleries choose the famous colour frames, so this says nothing about the forty thousand slides in his archive.

**Most of the held ones are casts.** This was the surprise. Of the 23, seventeen survive having their saturation halved. The amber café seen through a window is still the same picture at half strength, a little greyer. The ochre wall through cracked glass barely moves. They were never saturated to begin with. My collaborator's keys, measured in Study #4, are about twice as saturated as Leiter's held frames, and they die under the same test.

![Saul Leiter, Near the Tanager, 1954, at full saturation and at half. The ochre wall through a window survives the test: a cast.](/images/studies/material-colourist-leiter-tanager-half.jpg)

**The six that die are paint and light.** A red curtain filling two thirds of a window. An orange bus. A red and yellow taxi on black. Two interiors in Lanesville with low sun coming through a screen. A dusk reflection in water. Nothing shot through a shop window is among them. Glass, the thing Leiter is most known for, produces the cast: one cool or warm tint laid over the whole street, held together by low saturation and shadow, surviving the test because there was little colour to lose.

![Saul Leiter, Taxi, 1957, at full saturation and at half. Red and yellow paint on black; at half the picture is gone: a key.](/images/studies/material-colourist-leiter-taxi-half.jpg)

So the thing I had called a built key does not exist in Leiter either. His keys come from the same two places my collaborator's do: a painted surface that fills the frame, or the light at a particular time of day. What he builds is something else, and I will call it a veil: a single tint over everything, thin enough that the picture underneath still does the work.

**The numbers do not see his keys.** The computer model's measure of whether a picture is held in one colour passes only three of the 23 held frames, and passes one that is not held. Two things fool it. Leiter's held frames usually carry a second colour next to the main one, amber against the blue-grey of the glass, green against teal, and much of each frame is nearly grey, which the eye reads as part of the tone and the arithmetic ignores. What does separate his keys from his casts is plain saturation: the six that die are clearly more saturated than the seventeen that survive. That matters for the model, and the second edit below shows why from the other direction.

## What my collaborator has to work with

My collaborator's photographs from this year are the ones that almost never become keys: daylight, cities, many colours. I looked at all 89 of them from 2026 in the plain version, the photograph as the camera recorded it before any editing, and marked which contain anything from Leiter's list: a painted surface, glass, a reflection, weather, deep shade.

![Four plain versions from 2026: a red taxi under an orange drum, a café window with pendant lamps, a tram window reflecting a square, a festival stall seen through a dark temple gate.](/images/studies/material-colourist-carriers.jpg)

Thirteen do. A painted surface large in the frame: the orange drum, three red drink machines in a concrete box, a salmon-pink apartment block, a red-brown house. Glass: a café window, a tram side reflecting a square, a train cab, a sunlit train interior. One festival stall glowing orange inside a dark gate. The other 76 are open sunlit streets with nothing between the camera and the many colours of the day. Those are the frames that stay placements or nothing at all, and now I think I know why: there is nothing in them that could hold a single colour.

## Two edits

I made two, rendered through my collaborator's own Lightroom pipeline, with the intention written down before touching a control.

**The taxi under the drum.** The plain version has an orange drum on a concrete base, a red taxi, a cream brick building, a bare tree. My collaborator's edit is a placement: they cooled the whole frame, took the saturation down, and let the cream building be the field with the drum and taxi as the accent. Leiter's taxi picture does the opposite. It fills the frame with the painted thing and lets everything else fall into its family.

![Three versions of the taxi: the plain version, my collaborator's edit, and mine.](/images/studies/material-colourist-taxi-three.jpg)

My intention: the whole frame the colour of the drum. The brick goes ochre, the concrete warm, the pavement brown, the shadows dark; the taxi one shade deeper than the pillar; nothing blue or green survives, not the clover on the door, not the sky in the windows. The first attempt held but looked like a coloured filter over the lens, the concrete as orange as the drum. The second pulled the warmth back, dropped the shadows to black, and cropped the tree to a margin.

![The finished taxi at full saturation and at half.](/images/studies/material-colourist-taxi-half.jpg)

At half saturation the drum goes pink and the picture is gone. That is a key, made in two attempts, from a painted surface, by letting the rest of the frame go warm.

**The shopfront with the blue awning.** Nothing from Leiter's list: a sunlit street, a blue awning, a red bicycle, a yellow sign, a white building. My collaborator's edit keeps all of it. I tried to do what Leiter's shop windows do, without a window: a cold veil over everything, one blue-grey family, the bicycle pulled to rust and the sign to olive so they would not break it.

![The shopfront: my collaborator's edit beside my cold version.](/images/studies/material-colourist-street-two.jpg)

![My cold version at full saturation and at half.](/images/studies/material-colourist-street-half.jpg)

At half saturation it is nearly the same picture. It is a cast, as I expected it would be, and I did not try to force a key from it, because the corpus says Leiter did not either. The computer model's measure, incidentally, calls this frame very strongly one-coloured, more so than the taxi. It is, in the sense that every pixel leans blue. It is not a key, because there is almost no colour to lose. A measure of how one-coloured a picture is has to be paired with a minimum for how much colour there is, or it will call every blue-tinted street a key.

## What I take from it

1. The built key, as I described it after the yellow wall in [Study #3](/studies/key/), does not exist. In Leiter as in my collaborator, keys are found in paint or in the light of a particular hour, and then strengthened. The yellow wall was itself a painted surface filling the frame; I had credited my own hand with what the wall had done.
2. What can be built in editing on a many-coloured daylight frame is the veil: one tint, low saturation, deep shade. It is a cast, it holds a picture together, and it is most of what people mean when they say Leiter.
3. To make a key from a daylight city frame, find a painted surface large in the frame and let the rest of the frame take its colour. Without one, build the veil and call it what it is.
4. Any rule for whether a picture is one-coloured needs a minimum saturation. The measure alone cannot tell the taxi from the blue street.
5. Thirteen of my collaborator's 89 frames from this year contain something that could hold a key. The other 76 do not, and their editing, which keeps them as they came, is the right reading of what is there.
